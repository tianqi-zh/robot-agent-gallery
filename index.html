01 / A TEST OF THE BENCHMARK’S INSTRUCTIONS

The instruction should
communicate the goal.

Imagine a person operating a robot from a task instruction and live camera views. They have neither watched a demonstration video nor read the code that decides success. Can they infer the intended goal and reach a state the benchmark accepts?

That is the question we examine in LIBERO. GPT-6 serves as a proxy for this instruction-following human operator. It interprets the task, observes the scene, and controls the robot. The experiment tests whether the goal communicated through language and the visible scene is sufficient—without additional benchmark-specific guidance about what counts as completion.

GPT-6 is an imperfect proxy. It can misidentify an object, lose contact, or mistakenly announce that it is done. We therefore used human-in-the-loop verification of every agent-success / bench-failure video in the before-and-after comparison. A plausible completion accepted by human review remains a disagreement with the benchmark. An erroneous agent judgment is corrected before the alignment score is computed.

Our main subject is the benchmark’s language–vision–action alignment: does the instruction communicate the goal encoded by the evaluator? A low task success rate alone cannot answer that question, because failures can also come from the operator’s perception or control.

LIBERO was introduced to study knowledge transfer in lifelong robot learning, with language-conditioned tasks and human demonstrations. We examine 40 tasks from its Spatial, Object, Goal, and LIBERO-10 suites, with ten initial states each. The full collection contains 130 tasks. [LIBERO paper]

03 / MAKE THE INTENDED GOAL LEGIBLE

Clarify the instruction.
Keep the evaluator fixed.

The original comparison contains 47 completions accepted by human review but rejected by the benchmark, across nine tasks. We inspected the task definitions, demonstrations, and rollouts to understand the gap, then revised the instructions to communicate the intended goal more clearly.

This distinction matters: the authors used code and demonstrations to diagnose the benchmark; the policy never received those sources directly. It received the revised instruction and the same kind of live observations. The evaluator, task initialization, and control budget remained fixed.

The table gives the original and final tested instructions verbatim. Rates cover all ten episodes of each task. The two final revisions add a short spatial description to the original sentence.

TaskOriginal instructionFinal tested instructionNative successes
One revision was our mistake.

The first plate revision used an incorrect geometric offset: it confused the stove fixture origin with the visible burner center. All ten episodes still failed. We corrected that diagnosis and reran the task with the simple phrase “close to its front edge,” which achieved 2/10. The final instruction contains no numeric distance. That intermediate error is part of the record, not evidence that greater verbal precision always helps.

Changes were not uniformly beneficial. After the centering revision, observed native success on the black-bowl task fell from 5/10 to 4/10. These small, targeted reruns are descriptive evidence; they are not a held-out or statistically controlled estimate of a general prompting effect.

The code can be consistent while the language is incomplete.

A fixed geometric predicate makes evaluation reproducible. But an operator working from the instruction may see several plausible completions while the code accepts only one. A demonstration can reveal that convention—where exactly to place the book, how far to open the drawer—even when the sentence does not. Withholding demonstration videos lets us examine what the instruction itself communicates.

“Open” may hide an extension threshold; “on the plate” may require the object’s center to be within a small radius; “back” may name a particular compartment rather than any rearward bin. Making those distinctions observable gives us a cleaner test of the policy’s manipulation ability. [Native object-state checks]

05 / WHAT TRAINING MAY INHERIT

A demonstration can teach
more than its instruction says.

This is why demonstration videos are part of the information boundary in our experiment. They can make an unspoken benchmark convention learnable without making the accompanying instruction any clearer.

Suppose a strong generalist policy already maps everyday language to a broad range of reasonable actions. Post-training on demonstrations paired with underspecified instructions could reward a narrower, benchmark-specific convention: one asset identity, one precise placement, or one interpretation of a relational word.

Underspecified language↓One demonstrated interpretation↓Optimization toward that convention

Our concern is that this pressure could reduce transfer if those conventions conflict with useful prior behavior. But narrower supervision is not automatically harmful; consistent demonstrations can also teach valuable disambiguation and control. Whether the net effect damages a generalist policy is an empirical question.

This is a hypothesis, not a measured result of this audit. We updated no model weights and measured no post-training degradation. A direct test would compare matched demonstration sets with original versus clarified language, hold model initialization and training compute fixed, and evaluate both native performance and transfer to independently specified tasks.

For benchmark design, the immediate lesson is more modest: check whether the instruction, the demonstration, and the success predicate express the same observable goal before treating every rejected rollout as a policy deficiency.

06 / THE LIMITS OF THE HUMAN PROXY

GPT-6 can misunderstand.
It can also fail to execute.

Clearer language does not solve perception, contact dynamics, or long action sequences. The final plate task still fails eight times out of ten. Seven failures consume 496–500 control steps, repeatedly trying to establish useful contact or reposition the plate. One ends early with a false-complete judgment after confusing the left drawer cabinet with the stove on the right.

The plate r05 episode shows why the human check is essential. It was the only raw agent-success / bench-failure report in the final composite. Human review rejected its completion claim: the plate was moved toward the wrong fixture. Moving it in front of a cabinet does not satisfy “front of the stove,” even without consulting the benchmark’s success code. It now counts as agent failure / bench failure in the reviewed table. The original claim remains visible in the video record, so the correction can be inspected.

Using GPT-6 as a proxy does not make it a reliable robot operator. These tasks expose different limitations: recognizing the correct fixture, maintaining contact while pushing, avoiding nearby clutter, and completing the last state-changing action after several earlier subtasks. A fluent description of an intended plan is not evidence that the robot can reliably execute or verify it.

The bowl regression is another useful counterexample. A more explicit target can demand finer control, and ten stochastic episodes cannot tell us whether a small difference will persist. We keep that result visible alongside the improvements.

A BETTER QUESTION TO ASK

Did the robot miss the goal,
or did we fail to specify it?

Both happen. A useful evaluation should help us tell them apart. Publish interpretable success conditions, identify ambiguous referents, check demonstrations against their language, and report native success together with disagreement and human review of the disputed cases. Then failed videos can tell us more than a single score.

METHODS & LIMITS

A record you can inspect.

Evaluation protocol

All three stages requested gpt-6-astra with high reasoning through the same visual robot interface. Episodes used fixed task initial-state IDs and seeds 0–9, a 500-control-step budget, a 750-tool-call limit, and a 3,600-second wall-clock limit. The original stage used 10 workers, the first revision 8, and the final revision 4 on GPUs 0–3. Scheduling changed between stages; model calls are not guaranteed deterministic.

The task instruction supplied the goal. Live RGB views, robot proprioception, camera calibration, and generic control guidance supported execution. The policy had no access to demonstration videos or success-checker code, and received no hidden object poses or native success labels. No task-specific fine-tuning was performed. The environment still terminated automatically on native success. “Zero-shot” describes this evaluation protocol; it makes no claim about the model’s pretraining exposure.

Outcome accounting and human-in-the-loop review

Benchmark successes are accepted as agent successes even when native termination occurs before a finish declaration. The agent-failure / bench-success cell is therefore marked \. Five original and nine final-composite benchmark failures reached the 500-step limit without a finish declaration; the reporting rule assigns them to agent failure. That assignment is not an independent human judgment of their terminal images.

Human-in-the-loop verification covered videos where the original agent and benchmark judgments disagreed. The table and IAS use the resulting adjudicated labels. The correction log preserves each changed label, its original claim, the source episode, and the reason; raw finish records and native outcomes remain available separately. GPT-6 is a proxy for a human operator, not a validated measure of human performance. The reviewers adjudicated disputed completion claims; they did not operate the robot in a controlled human baseline. A 100% IAS means no disagreement remains under this review and reporting rule, not that every task succeeded or every video received human review.

Selection, revisions, and uncertainty

We selected nine tasks after finding disagreements in the baseline, so the revised subset is intentionally enriched for such failures. Revision 1 reran 90 episodes; revision 2 reran all 20 episodes of Goal t05 and LIBERO-10 t05. The after comparison uses the latest instruction stage for every initial state of a task, including failures. It does not pool all rerun attempts or choose the best individual outcome. Both rerun batches completed without timeouts, infrastructure errors, or retries. The original batch had four infrastructure-error attempts, each followed by one valid attempt; no slot had multiple valid outcomes to choose from.

The revisions were informed by source and demonstration inspection. This is a diagnostic intervention, not a blind benchmark evaluation or a causal estimate of language quality. Ten episodes per task, shared initial states, and stochastic model outputs limit generalization. Paired clips illustrate particular mechanisms; full task counts provide the quantitative context.
