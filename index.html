01 / WHAT ARE WE MEASURING?

A goal should be inferable
from the task itself.

Imagine a competent person operating the robot from the same camera views and instruction, without reading the simulator’s success code. Could they tell which object to move, where it should go, and when the task is done? That is the intuition behind this audit.

Failure alone does not answer that question. A person or a policy can understand the goal and execute it badly. The interesting cases are narrower: an apparently reasonable completion is rejected because the instruction leaves an object identity, spatial reference, or required tolerance unstated.

LIBERO was introduced to study knowledge transfer in lifelong robot learning, with language-conditioned tasks and human demonstrations. We examine 40 tasks from its Spatial, Object, Goal, and LIBERO-10 suites, with ten fixed initial states each—not the entire 130-task collection. [LIBERO paper]

Our focus is the agreement between language, the visible scene, and the native success condition. We use an agent’s explicit completion judgment to find candidate disagreements, then inspect videos and task definitions to distinguish ambiguous goals from genuine policy mistakes. No human study was conducted; the thought experiment motivates the audit, but does not establish what a human would achieve.

Here, “agent success” means an explicit visually_complete assessment from the agent’s finish action. We read that explicit report; we never infer it from the simulator’s label. An absent assessment stays unknown.

The full comparison preserves 310 original episodes and replaces all 90 episodes from the nine revised tasks.

These are observed disagreement scores, not benchmark success rates.

Native success often ends an episode before the agent can submit its own finish assessment. Those successes appear in the unknown row. A policy that always declares failure could score 100% on this metric while accomplishing nothing. Read it alongside native success, missing assessments, and the videos.

A higher score means fewer observed false-complete judgments under this policy and protocol. We propose it as a diagnostic of language–vision–action alignment; these runs do not validate it as an intrinsic property of a benchmark or a substitute for human judgment.

The “after” result is a composite, not a fresh 400-episode run: 310 unchanged original episodes, 70 episodes from the first instruction revision, and 20 from the final revision of two tasks. Every revised task uses all ten original initial states. No episode was selected because it succeeded.

03 / THE INTERVENTION

Change the words.
Keep the success condition.

We revised the nine tasks containing the original 47 explicit false-complete judgments. The authors inspected benchmark definitions, demonstrations, and failed rollouts to write the revisions. The policy received the revised language; it did not receive those definitions, demonstrations, or hidden target coordinates.

The table gives the original and final tested instructions verbatim. Rates cover all ten episodes of each task. The two final revisions add a short spatial description to the original sentence.

TaskOriginal instructionFinal tested instructionNative successes
One revision was our mistake.

The first plate revision used an incorrect geometric offset: it confused the stove fixture origin with the visible burner center. All ten episodes still failed. We corrected that diagnosis and reran the task with the simple phrase “close to its front edge,” which achieved 2/10. The final instruction contains no numeric distance. That intermediate error is part of the record, not evidence that greater verbal precision always helps.

Changes were not uniformly beneficial. After the centering revision, observed native success on the black-bowl task fell from 5/10 to 4/10. These small, targeted reruns are descriptive evidence; they are not a held-out or statistically controlled estimate of a general prompting effect.

The code can be consistent while the language is incomplete.

A fixed geometric predicate is useful for reproducible evaluation. The problem arises when a natural-language phrase permits several visually plausible states and only one hidden convention is accepted. In these cases, a demonstration can agree with the code while teaching a narrower goal than its accompanying sentence specifies.

“Open” may hide an extension threshold; “on the plate” may require the object’s center to be within a small radius; “back” may name a particular compartment rather than any rearward bin. Making those distinctions observable gives us a cleaner test of the policy’s manipulation ability. [Native object-state checks]

05 / WHY THIS MATTERS BEYOND A LEADERBOARD

What does the demonstration
actually teach?

Suppose a strong generalist policy already maps everyday language to a broad range of reasonable actions. Post-training on demonstrations paired with underspecified instructions could reward a narrower, benchmark-specific convention: one asset identity, one precise placement, or one interpretation of a relational word.

Underspecified language↓One demonstrated interpretation↓Optimization toward that convention

Our concern is that this pressure could reduce transfer if those conventions conflict with useful prior behavior. But narrower supervision is not automatically harmful; consistent demonstrations can also teach valuable disambiguation and control. Whether the net effect damages a generalist policy is an empirical question.

This is a hypothesis, not a measured result of this audit. We updated no model weights and measured no post-training degradation. A direct test would compare matched demonstration sets with original versus clarified language, hold model initialization and training compute fixed, and evaluate both native performance and transfer to independently specified tasks.

For benchmark design, the immediate lesson is more modest: check whether the instruction, the demonstration, and the success predicate express the same observable goal before treating every rejected rollout as a policy deficiency.

06 / THE POLICY HAS REAL LIMITS

GPT-6 as policy
is not a universal robot policy.

Clearer language does not solve perception, contact dynamics, or long action sequences. The final plate task still fails eight times out of ten. Seven failures consume 496–500 control steps, repeatedly trying to establish useful contact or reposition the plate. One ends early with a false-complete judgment after confusing the left drawer cabinet with the stove on the right.

That last error is also the single explicit false-complete judgment left in the after composite. It is a policy grounding mistake, illustrating why our score cannot automatically attribute every disagreement to the benchmark.

These tasks expose different limitations: recognizing the correct fixture, maintaining contact while pushing, avoiding nearby clutter, and completing the last state-changing action after several earlier subtasks. A fluent description of an intended plan is not evidence that the robot can reliably execute or verify it.

The bowl regression is another useful counterexample. A more explicit target can demand finer control, and ten stochastic episodes cannot tell us whether a small difference will persist. We keep that result visible alongside the improvements.

A BETTER QUESTION TO ASK

Did the robot miss the goal,
or did we fail to specify it?

Both happen. A useful evaluation should help us tell them apart. Publish interpretable success conditions, identify ambiguous referents, check demonstrations against their language, and report native success together with disagreement and assessment coverage. Then failed videos can tell us more than a single score.

METHODS & LIMITS

A record you can inspect.

Evaluation protocol

All three stages requested gpt-6-astra with high reasoning through the same visual robot interface. Episodes used fixed task initial-state IDs and seeds 0–9, a 500-control-step budget, a 750-tool-call limit, and a 3,600-second wall-clock limit. The original stage used 10 workers, the first revision 8, and the final revision 4 on GPUs 0–3. Scheduling changed between stages; model calls are not guaranteed deterministic.

The policy received RGB views, robot state, and camera calibration, with no task-specific fine-tuning, training demonstrations, hidden object poses, or native success labels. The environment still terminated automatically on native success. “Zero-shot” describes this evaluation protocol; it makes no claim about the model’s pretraining exposure.

Outcome and assessment accounting

The original 400 episodes contain 322 native successes and 78 failures. Explicit assessments account for 47 visually complete and 26 unable-to-continue episodes; 327 have no independent assessment. The final composite contains 357 native successes and 43 failures, with 1 visually complete, 33 unable-to-continue, and 366 unknown assessments. Every explicit completion judgment here occurs on a native failure.

Five original native failures and nine final-composite native failures lack an assessment. Unknown is not equivalent to success or failure. The proposed score therefore measures observed disagreement and is sensitive to the agent’s reporting behavior and the automatic termination protocol.

Selection, revisions, and uncertainty

We selected nine tasks after finding disagreements in the baseline, so the revised subset is intentionally enriched for such failures. Revision 1 reran 90 episodes; revision 2 reran all 20 episodes of Goal t05 and LIBERO-10 t05. The after comparison uses the latest instruction stage for every initial state of a task, including failures. It does not pool all rerun attempts or choose the best individual outcome. Both rerun batches completed without timeouts, infrastructure errors, or retries. The original batch had four infrastructure-error attempts, each followed by one valid attempt; no slot had multiple valid outcomes to choose from.

The revisions were informed by source and demonstration inspection. This is a diagnostic intervention, not a blind benchmark evaluation or a causal estimate of language quality. Ten episodes per task, shared initial states, and stochastic model outputs limit generalization. Paired clips illustrate particular mechanisms; full task counts provide the quantitative context.
