01 / A TEST OF THE BENCHMARK’S INSTRUCTIONS

The instruction should
communicate the goal.

Imagine a person operating a robot from a task instruction and live camera views. They have neither watched a demonstration video nor read the code that decides success. Can they infer the intended goal and reach a state the benchmark accepts?

That is the question we examine in two settings: LIBERO, where we edited underspecified task sentences, and RoboTwin, where we exposed public goal criteria derived from benchmark success conditions. GPT-6 serves as a proxy for this instruction-following human operator. It interprets the task, observes the scene, and controls the robot. The experiment tests whether the goal communicated through language and the visible scene is sufficient—without additional benchmark-specific guidance about what counts as completion.

The agent’s judgment and the benchmark’s verdict answer different questions. The agent asks whether the observed result fulfills the instruction. The benchmark checks a particular condition in the simulator. When those judgments disagree, the instruction may have left part of that condition unstated.

Our main subject is the benchmark’s language–vision–action alignment: does the instruction communicate the goal encoded by the evaluator? A low task success rate alone cannot answer that question, because failures can also come from the operator’s perception or control.

LIBERO was introduced to study knowledge transfer in lifelong robot learning, with language-conditioned tasks and human demonstrations. We examine 40 tasks from its Spatial, Object, Goal, and LIBERO-10 suites, with ten initial states each. RoboTwin adds 50 dual-arm manipulation tasks, ten rollouts per task, with episode-specific object variants and instructions. [LIBERO paper] · [RoboTwin gallery]

02 / TWO JUDGMENTS OF THE SAME OUTCOME

The agent considers it done.
The benchmark disagrees.

We focus on episodes in which the agent considers the task complete, but the benchmark judges it a failure. This describes a difference between two judgments; it does not by itself establish that the agent executed the task incorrectly.

A check on the agent’s interpretation.

We manually reviewed every video in the before-and-after comparison where the agent claimed completion but the benchmark reported failure. All disagreements retained in the tables were confirmed to follow a reasonable reading of the task instruction. One claim was corrected: in the final plate r05 episode, the agent mistook a cabinet for the stove. The tables and IAS include that correction; the original claim remains in the assessment records.

03 / MAKE THE INTENDED GOAL LEGIBLE

Clarify the instruction.
Keep the evaluator fixed.

The original comparison contains 47 episodes across nine tasks in which the agent considers the instruction fulfilled but the benchmark judges the outcome a failure. We inspected the task definitions, demonstrations, and rollouts to understand the gap, then revised the instructions to communicate the intended goal more clearly.

This distinction matters: the authors used code and demonstrations to diagnose the benchmark; the policy never received those sources directly. It received the revised instruction and the same kind of live observations. The evaluator, task initialization, and control budget remained fixed.

We deliberately limited the GPT → language → GPT channel. For the improved instructions shown below, the agent refining the language was restricted to small edits to the original sentence, expressed in simple, natural language. It could add a cue such as “close to its front edge,” but not exact coordinates, centimeter-level target positions, or detailed control instructions. This limits the information passed to the robot-control agent, which still has to interpret the scene and work out how to act. The aim is to test whether a modest clarification of the task helps, while keeping the instruction from becoming a detailed handoff between two GPT agents.

The table gives the original and improved instructions verbatim, with added or changed wording in bold. Rates cover all ten episodes of each task. The final plate and book revisions each add a short spatial description to the original sentence.

A clearer goal can improve the judgment
without improving the success rate.

Instruction refinement changes what the agent understands as “done.” It does not automatically improve perception, contact control, or the ability to reach that state. A task can remain difficult—even become harder—while the agent’s completion judgment becomes more consistent with the benchmark’s verdict.

Spatial t04 makes this distinction visible. After “on the plate” became “in the center of the plate,” benchmark success fell from 5/10 to 4/10, yet episodes the agent considered complete and the benchmark judged failures fell from 3 to 0. For the same initial state r01, the benchmark judged both attempts failures: the agent declared completion under the original instruction, but reported that it could not continue under the improved instruction. Four of the six final failures had that explicit assessment; two exhausted the control budget without claiming completion.

The plate task also remains hard: benchmark success rises only from 0/10 to 2/10, while completion claims rejected by the benchmark fall from 9 to 1 before the correction noted above. Clearer language gives the agent a more specific goal to check, even when it cannot reliably achieve it.

These examples support reading task success and completion judgment together. The original placements could satisfy the original wording; the improved wording communicates a narrower target closer to the evaluator’s condition. Ten episodes per task describe the observed change, rather than establish a general improvement in the agent’s judgment or manipulation ability.

The code can be consistent while the language is incomplete.

A fixed geometric predicate makes evaluation reproducible. But an operator working from the instruction may see several plausible completions while the code accepts only one. A demonstration can reveal that convention—where exactly to place the book, how far to open the drawer—even when the sentence does not. Withholding demonstration videos lets us examine what the instruction itself communicates.

“Open” may hide an extension threshold; “on the plate” may require the object’s center to be within a small radius; “back” may name a particular compartment rather than any rearward bin. Making those distinctions observable gives us a cleaner test of the policy’s manipulation ability. [Native object-state checks]

05 / ROBOTWIN AS A SECOND BENCHMARK

Episode-specific instructions
need episode-specific goals.

RoboTwin is harder to audit with one hand-written sentence per task. The benchmark generates episode-specific instructions from seed-dependent object variants: the same task may mention different colors, object roles, sides of the table, or target containers across ten rollouts. A useful fix therefore cannot be a one-off rewrite of a single instruction. It has to improve the instruction-generation chain.

For the local rerun we kept the evaluator fixed and added a public goal spec derived from each task’s success condition. The policy still did not receive hidden object poses, segmentation, rewards, expert trajectories, or success flags. It received natural-language criteria such as the required side, object identity, orientation, contact, containment, or ordering condition, appended to the episode instruction as observable checks.

Using the same IAS definition, RoboTwin improves from the original NVIDIA run to the goal-spec rerun:

The key shift is not just native success. It is the drop in agent success / benchmark false cases: outcomes that looked complete to the agent under the public instruction, but failed the hidden checker. On the overlapping terminal episodes, the rerun fixes many of the original disagreements while introducing a smaller number of new ones. This is exactly the failure mode IAS is intended to expose.

The gallery view below shows the playable scored subset of the rerun: 479 videos from 500 planned rollouts, with 396 native successes and 83 playable failures. The IAS table uses 498 terminal results; two budget-limited episodes did not produce terminal results and are excluded from that table. RoboTwin alignment summary JSON · RoboTwin gallery

Why this is a chain-level fix.

RoboTwin instructions are generated after the scene seed and object variant are chosen. Improving a single English sentence is not enough; the generator must expose the success condition in public, observable language for each episode. That is why the rerun adds task-level goal criteria and episode-specific fields instead of patching one rollout at a time.

06 / WHAT TRAINING MAY INHERIT

A demonstration can teach
more than its instruction says.

This is why demonstration videos are part of the information boundary in our experiment. They can make an unspoken benchmark convention learnable without making the accompanying instruction any clearer.

Suppose a strong generalist policy already maps everyday language to a broad range of reasonable actions. Post-training on demonstrations paired with underspecified instructions could reward a narrower, benchmark-specific convention: one asset identity, one precise placement, or one interpretation of a relational word.

Underspecified language↓One demonstrated interpretation↓Optimization toward that convention

Our concern is that this pressure could reduce transfer if those conventions conflict with useful prior behavior. But narrower supervision is not automatically harmful; consistent demonstrations can also teach valuable disambiguation and control. Whether the net effect damages a generalist policy is an empirical question.

This is a hypothesis, not a measured result of this audit. We updated no model weights and measured no post-training degradation. A direct test would compare matched demonstration sets with original versus clarified language, hold model initialization and training compute fixed, and evaluate both native performance and transfer to independently specified tasks.

For benchmark design, the immediate lesson is more modest: check whether the instruction, the demonstration, and the success predicate express the same observable goal before treating every rejected rollout as a policy deficiency.

07 / THE LIMITS OF THE HUMAN PROXY

GPT-6 can misunderstand.
It can also fail to execute.

Clearer language does not solve perception, contact dynamics, or long action sequences. The final plate task still fails eight times out of ten. Seven failures consume 496–500 control steps, repeatedly trying to establish useful contact or reposition the plate. One ends early with a mistaken completion claim after confusing the left drawer cabinet with the stove on the right.

RoboTwin shows the same limitation at a larger scale. The goal-spec rerun improves alignment, but failures remain in tasks that require long bimanual coordination, stable grasping, precise stacking, full containment, or reliable object identification. In plate r05, the LIBERO agent considers the task complete after moving the plate toward the wrong fixture. Moving it in front of a cabinet does not satisfy “front of the stove,” even without consulting the benchmark’s success code. This is a perception error, distinct from interpreting an underspecified spatial goal.

Using GPT-6 as a proxy does not make it a reliable robot operator. These tasks expose different limitations: recognizing the correct fixture, maintaining contact while pushing, avoiding nearby clutter, and completing the last state-changing action after several earlier subtasks. A fluent description of an intended plan is not evidence that the robot can reliably execute or verify it.

The bowl regression is another useful counterexample. A more explicit target can demand finer control, and ten stochastic episodes cannot tell us whether a small difference will persist. We keep that result visible alongside the improvements.

A BETTER QUESTION TO ASK

Did the robot miss the goal,
or did we fail to specify it?

Both happen. A useful evaluation should help us tell them apart. Publish interpretable success conditions, identify ambiguous referents, check demonstrations against their language, and report the benchmark’s success rate alongside the agent’s judgment of completion. Then failed videos can tell us more than a single score.

METHODS & LIMITS

A record you can inspect.

Evaluation protocol

All stages requested gpt-6-astra through the same visual robot interface. LIBERO episodes used fixed task initial-state IDs and seeds 0–9, a 500-control-step budget, a 750-tool-call limit, and a 3,600-second wall-clock limit. The original LIBERO stage used 10 workers, the first revision 8, and the final revision 4 on GPUs 0–3. RoboTwin used the NVIDIA Responses API route for azure/openai/gpt-6-astra, 50 official tasks, and ten requested rollouts per task.

The task instruction supplied the goal. Live RGB views, robot proprioception, camera calibration, and generic control guidance supported execution. The policy had no access to demonstration videos or success-checker code, and received no hidden object poses or native success labels. For RoboTwin, the goal-spec rerun adds public natural-language criteria derived from success checks, but not private state, reward, segmentation, expert trajectories, or native success. No task-specific fine-tuning was performed. The environment still terminated automatically on native success. “Zero-shot” describes this evaluation protocol; it makes no claim about the model’s pretraining exposure.

How completion judgments are counted

The displayed judgments use the assessment correction described above. Every outcome the benchmark judges successful is counted as complete on the agent side, including episodes terminated before a finish declaration. For outcomes the benchmark judges failures, retained explicit completion claims count as “agent considers complete”; rejected claims, explicit inability to continue, and episodes without a completion claim count as “agent does not consider complete.” Five original and nine final-composite failures reached the 500-step limit without a finish declaration. This last assignment is an accounting rule, not an explicit judgment made by the agent.

IAS measures disagreement under that rule, not standalone prediction accuracy. A policy that never claims completion on a failed rollout can score 100% IAS despite never completing a task. Read IAS together with native success and the recorded behavior. GPT-6 is a proxy for a human operator; we did not measure human robot-control performance in a controlled baseline.

Selection, revisions, and uncertainty

We selected nine LIBERO tasks after finding disagreements in the baseline, so the revised subset is intentionally enriched for such failures. Revision 1 reran 90 episodes; revision 2 reran all 20 episodes of Goal t05 and LIBERO-10 t05. The LIBERO after comparison uses the latest instruction stage for every initial state of a task, including failures. It does not pool all rerun attempts or choose the best individual outcome. Both rerun batches completed without timeouts, infrastructure errors, or retries. The original batch had four infrastructure-error attempts, each followed by one valid attempt; no slot had multiple valid outcomes to choose from.

The RoboTwin after comparison is a fresh goal-spec rerun, not a hand-selected subset. It contains 498 terminal results from 500 planned episodes; two episodes remained missing after API budget exhaustion. The gallery shows 479 playable scored videos because simulator/API infrastructure errors are retained in provenance rather than presented as scored media.

The revisions were informed by source and demonstration inspection. This is a diagnostic intervention, not a blind benchmark evaluation or a causal estimate of language quality. Ten episodes per task, shared initial states, and stochastic model outputs limit generalization. Paired clips illustrate particular mechanisms; full task counts provide the quantitative context.
